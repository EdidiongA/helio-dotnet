# DC wiring, fusing and protection

DC cables between the battery and inverter carry high current at low voltage, so voltage drop and heating are the governing constraints. For a 5 kVA inverter on a 48 V bank the continuous current is about 105 A, and surge current can reach 200 A. Use 35 mm2 copper for runs up to 2 m and 50 mm2 for runs up to 4 m to keep voltage drop under 2 percent. Keep battery cable runs as short as physically possible.

Every battery bank needs an over-current protection device within 150 mm of the positive terminal. Use a Class T fuse or a DC-rated breaker with an interrupt rating that exceeds the battery's short-circuit current; lithium banks can deliver several thousand amps into a dead short. An AC breaker is not acceptable on the DC side because it cannot extinguish a DC arc.

PV strings need a DC isolator rated for the string's open-circuit voltage at the coldest expected temperature, because open-circuit voltage rises as temperature falls. A string of ten panels with a 49 V open-circuit rating at 25 degrees reaches about 520 V at 10 degrees, so a 600 V isolator is the minimum. String fuses are required when three or more strings are paralleled, sized at 1.56 times the short-circuit current.

Surge protection devices on both the DC and AC sides are essential in Lagos because of lightning density in the rainy season. Type 2 devices are the normal choice for a residential installation; connect them with the shortest possible leads to the earth bar.

All metal frames, mounting rails and the inverter chassis must be bonded to a single earth electrode. Separate earth rods that are not bonded together create a potential difference during a lightning strike and are a common cause of damaged inverters.
