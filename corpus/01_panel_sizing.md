# Sizing a solar array for a household load

A household load profile is the starting point for any array size. Add up the watt-hours each appliance uses per day: a 150 W refrigerator running a 40 percent duty cycle uses about 1.44 kWh per day, a 65 W laptop used for eight hours uses 0.52 kWh, and ten 9 W LED lamps for six hours use 0.54 kWh. A typical two-bedroom home in Lagos with a refrigerator, fans, lighting, a television and phone charging lands between 4 and 6 kWh per day before any air conditioning.

Peak sun hours, not daylight hours, determine how much a panel produces. Lagos averages about 4.0 to 4.5 peak sun hours per day across the year, dropping to around 3.0 during the heaviest rainy months of June to September and rising above 5.0 in the dry season. Harmattan dust in December and January can reduce output by 10 to 20 percent until panels are cleaned.

The sizing rule is array watts = daily load in watt-hours divided by peak sun hours, divided by a system efficiency factor of about 0.75. For a 5 kWh daily load at 4 peak sun hours, that gives 5000 / 4 / 0.75, which is roughly 1,670 W of panels. Round up to the nearest panel count: with 450 W modules that is four panels, giving 1,800 W.

Oversizing the array relative to the inverter by 20 to 30 percent is common practice because panels rarely reach their nameplate rating in real conditions. Heat alone costs about 0.35 percent of output for every degree Celsius above 25 degrees at the cell, and cells in Lagos routinely run at 55 to 65 degrees on a clear afternoon.
